\begin{abstract}
Delivery operators must predict a drone's energy use before takeoff, because running out of battery mid-flight
usually means losing the aircraft. Choudhry et al.~\cite{choudhry2021} showed that a temporal convolutional
network (TCN) trained on flight logs predicts quadrotor power better than a physics model, but they only tested it
on flights whose payload, speed and altitude also appear in training. We ported their models to PyTorch,
reproduced their results, and stress-tested them on held-out operating conditions. To separate the cost of an
unseen condition from the cost of having less training data, we compare each held-out model with a control trained
on the same number of randomly chosen flights, and score both only on flights neither model saw. For the small TCN, holding out a
payload, a speed range or an altitude raises power error by at most 0.4 percentage points (7--10\% MAPE), and
the model still beats the Liu physics model (11--14\%) everywhere. Speed turns out not to be a real
extrapolation in this dataset, so we added a wind split built from the raw anemometer and GNSS logs. Both TCN
sizes lose about one point only on a new route and on windy flights (+0.8 and +1.2 MAPE points for wind).
Retrained LSTMs are unreliable: 16 of 30 runs collapse to a constant prediction, which we trace to dead ReLU units
in the output head.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A drone's power draw depends on its speed, climb rate, tilt, payload and the wind. Published energy models give
substantially different estimates even for the same drone and conditions~\cite{zhang2021}. Choudhry et
al.~\cite{choudhry2021} instead learn power directly from flight data, but split flights at random, so every test
flight reuses payload, speed and altitude levels seen in training.

\textbf{Motivation.} Operators and route planners need energy estimates they can trust before takeoff. A model
that is accurate on familiar flights but wrong on new ones gives false confidence exactly when risk is highest.

\textbf{Objectives.} As in our proposal, we (i) measure how the published models degrade on held-out operating
conditions, (ii) combine physics with a neural network, and (iii) add calibrated uncertainty (target: 90\%
prediction intervals with $\approx$90\% empirical coverage). Our midterm results (Section~\ref{sec:results})
change objective (ii). We had planned to beat the TCN's error on held-out payload, speed and altitude, but the TCN
barely degrades there. The hybrid will instead target data efficiency and genuinely new conditions (route, wind),
where the gaps we measure are larger.

\section{Related work and background}
\textbf{Deep energy models.} Choudhry et al.~\cite{choudhry2021} predict power at each time step from a 20-step
window of airspeed, its components, vertical speed and angle of attack, plus air density and payload. Their best
TCN reaches 9.06\% test MAPE versus 12.70\% for the Liu physics model~\cite{liu2017}, and they score the energy
distribution from Monte Carlo simulation with Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR). We use their
code~\cite{cvarcode}, metrics (power MAPE and per-regime energy error) and baselines, so our numbers are directly
comparable. \textbf{Physics models.} Liu et al.~\cite{liu2017} model power as induced, profile and parasitic terms
with five fitted constants. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2021} show that physics models disagree widely, which
motivates learning from data. \textbf{Dataset.} Rodrigues et al.~\cite{rodrigues2021} released the flight logs
(DJI Matrice 100, Penn Hills, PA) used by~\cite{choudhry2021}. \textbf{Uncertainty.} For the second half we draw
on deep ensembles~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017} and Monte Carlo dropout~\cite{gal2016}.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Models}
All networks map a window $x_{t-19:t}\in\mathbb{R}^{20\times5}$ of time-varying inputs (airspeed $x$/$y$
components, vertical speed, angle of attack, airspeed) and two static inputs (air density, payload) to the power
$\hat y_t$ at the last step (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The TCN backbone is a stack of residual blocks of two causal
dilated convolutions (kernel 2, dilations 1--16, ReLU), and its last-step features are concatenated with the
static inputs. The LSTM backbone has three stacked layers and carries its state across a flight (stateful,
batch size 1). Both feed a head of Linear$\to$ReLU$\to$Linear (10 hidden units).

\subsection{Dataset and splits}
We use the authors' preprocessed flights~\cite{cvarcode}: 187 flights of a DJI Matrice 100 at 5\,Hz, 179 on the
main triangular route covering every combination of payload (0/250/500\,g), speed (4--12\,m/s) and altitude
(25--100\,m), and 8 on other routes.\footnote{Our proposal quoted 195 + 10 flights from the full dataset
release; 187 of them have preprocessed data. We take route labels from the release's
\texttt{parameters.csv}~\cite{rodrigues2021}, which corrects two flights that the authors' flight sheet mislabels.} The target is battery voltage $\times$ current. Inputs are min--max normalized
with statistics from the training and validation flights (payload and density are left raw), and training samples
batches of 32 overlapping 20-step windows from training flights only (TCN; the stateful LSTMs use batch size 1).

Besides the paper's random split (114/38/35 train/val/test flights), each held-out split puts every flight with one
condition in the test set (Table~\ref{tab:splits}) and draws the validation set (25\%, seed 42) from the
remaining flights, so model selection never sees the held-out condition. Condition splits use main-route flights
only, so off-route flights appear only in \texttt{route}.

\textbf{Wind split.} The preprocessed data has no wind, so we estimate it from the raw logs~\cite{rodrigues2021}.
The anemometer measures airflow past the drone. In cruise, the wind is the ground velocity minus that airflow:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{v}_g - s\,(\sin a, \cos a), \qquad a = \alpha + \delta ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}_g$ is the body-frame velocity rotated by the INS heading into (east, north), $s$ and $\alpha$
are the anemometer speed and the compass direction the air comes from, and $\delta=-9.4^\circ$ is the magnetic
declination at the site in 2019 (WMM2015). We validated these conventions on the data: they make the wind
consistent between the legs of the route, while the alternative readings of the angle disagree 4--7$\times$ more. The flight's wind is
its mean over cruise. \texttt{wind} holds out flights above 4.34\,m/s, the windiest 20\% of the main route.

\subsection{Evaluation}
Following~\cite{choudhry2021}, the power error of flight $i$ with $T_i$ scored steps, measured power $y_t$ and
prediction $\hat y_t$, and its energy error over the flight's regimes $r$ (take-off, cruise, turns, landing,
found by change-point detection on pitch) are
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MAPE}_i = \frac{100\%}{T_i}\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}\left|\frac{y_t-\hat y_t}{y_t}\right|, \qquad
  \mathrm{RE}_i = \frac{100\%}{R_i}\sum_{r=1}^{R_i}\left|\frac{E_r-\hat E_r}{E_r}\right|,
\end{equation}
where $E_r$ is the Simpson-integrated energy of regime $r$. Both are averaged over flights; the first 19 steps of a flight
are not scored (no full window). We add two controls for a fair stress test. (1) \textbf{Size-matched control}: for every held-out split, a model trained on a random subset of the
random split's training flights of the same size, so the comparison isolates the unseen condition from having less
data. For \texttt{wind}, the random split already trains on most windy flights, which would leave only 7 for
comparison; its control instead tests on a random half of the windy flights and may train on the other half.
(2) \textbf{Unseen flights only}: the held-out model and its control are scored on the held-out flights that
\emph{neither} trained or validated on ($n$ in Table~\ref{tab:splits}). We report
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{gap} = \overline{\mathrm{MAPE}}_{\text{held-out}} - \overline{\mathrm{MAPE}}_{\text{control}},
\end{equation}
averaged over 3 seeds (mean $\pm$ std over seeds).

\subsection{Loss and optimization}
All networks minimize the mean squared error of normalized power. TCNs use Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$,
$\epsilon=10^{-7}$) with batch size 32; LSTMs use RMSprop (learning rate $10^{-3}$, $\rho=0.9$) with batch size 1. We train for 30
epochs; the authors' models were trained for 100, and 30 epochs already matches them for the TCNs and the s-LSTM
(Table~\ref{tab:repro}).
\footnote{Our proposal stated a learning rate of 0.002. The released code passes the optimizer by name, so Keras
uses its default of $10^{-3}$; our port matches the released code.}
The final-epoch checkpoint is evaluated.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Baseline reproduction}
Bill ported the authors' Keras TCN and LSTM~\cite{cvarcode} to PyTorch and converted their checkpoints (output
difference $<4\cdot10^{-7}$). The converted models reproduce the paper's power MAPE to within 0.01 points
(Table~\ref{tab:repro}). The energy error matches for Liu and the LSTMs but is 0.2--0.4 points higher for the TCNs.
Running the authors' own TensorFlow code gives the same small differences, so we attribute them to library versions
in the energy computation, not to the port.
Retrained from scratch, both TCNs match the paper over 3 seeds (s-TCN 8.86$\pm$0.26\% vs 9.13\%, b-TCN
8.92$\pm$0.16\% vs 9.06\% test MAPE), and so does the s-LSTM (12.42\% vs 12.36\%, one seed). The retrained b-LSTM
does not (16.80\% vs 11.47\%), for the reason in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline} shows the
s-TCN's training curves and example predictions.

We trained both TCNs on every held-out split and its control with 3 seeds (Table~\ref{tab:splits}; 78 runs). Two of the
splits are nested speed splits (train on 4--8 or 4--6\,m/s only), which we added after \texttt{speed12} showed no gap.
The LSTMs were trained on \texttt{wind} and its control with 3 seeds, and with one seed on the other splits (b-LSTM all
eleven, s-LSTM seven). Liu is evaluated on the same flights as a physics reference.

\textbf{Holding out a grid condition barely hurts the TCNs} (Table~\ref{tab:gaps}, Fig.~\ref{fig:gaps}). For the
s-TCN, holding out a payload, speed range or altitude costs at most 0.4 MAPE points. This is the same size as the
spread between seeds; only \texttt{alt100} is consistent across seeds. Extrapolating to 500\,g costs only slightly
more than interpolating to 250\,g. The b-TCN, with 15$\times$ the parameters, behaves the same way: within
$\pm$0.3 points, except \texttt{speed8-12} (+0.90 MAPE but $-$0.03 RE, so it does not carry over to energy). Both
TCNs beat Liu on every split, by 2.0--4.6 points. We do not report LSTM gaps, because most retrained LSTMs collapsed
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\textbf{Speed is not an extrapolation in this dataset} (failure analysis). We expected the largest gap on speed,
because parasitic power grows with airspeed. Even \texttt{speed8-12}, trained only on 4--6\,m/s flights, shows
no gap (Fig.~\ref{fig:gaps}, right). The reason is in the data, not the model. Mean flight power is nearly flat
across commanded speeds (521, 521, 522, 523, 535\,W at 4--12\,m/s), because take-off, climb, hover and landing look
the same at every speed. In addition, the network sees \emph{measured} airspeed, which includes wind: the
4--6\,m/s training flights already reach airspeeds of 11.9\,m/s (99th percentile) and 14.1\,m/s (max), versus
18.1\,m/s for the test flights. A held-out speed is therefore mostly inside the training input range. This
finding motivated the wind split, which shifts the airspeed input directly.

\textbf{New route.} \texttt{route} has the largest gap (+1.08 MAPE, +2.30 RE), but it is not a clean
comparison. All off-route flights are in the random split's test set, so the control has never seen them either.

\textbf{Wind is a real shift.} Unlike \texttt{route}, \texttt{wind} has a control that has seen the condition (it
trains on 12 windy flights). Both TCNs are worse on unseen windy flights, by +0.77$\pm$0.33 (s-TCN) and
+1.22$\pm$0.20 (b-TCN) MAPE points and +1.4 / +1.8 RE points: 2--6 times the combined seed spread, and larger than any
grid split. Windy flights are also harder in general: even the control scores 10.7--10.8\% on them, versus 7--10\% on
the grid splits. Training on some windy flights therefore helps, but does not remove the difficulty. One caveat: wind
is clustered by flight date. Half of the 36 windy flights come from two days (10 and 8 flights), so the split also
shifts date-dependent factors such as battery wear and temperature.

\textbf{Ablation (training-set size).} The controls share the random split's test flights, so they also show what
less data costs. Test MAPE over 3 seeds is 8.86$\pm$0.26 with all 114 training flights, 8.64$\pm$0.13 with 87,
10.33$\pm$0.86 with 78 and 8.88$\pm$0.21 with 52. Halving the training set costs nothing on average. The one bad
subset (78 flights, the worst on every seed) suggests that \emph{which} flights are in training matters more than
how many. A data-efficiency study therefore has to go below 52 flights and average over several subsets.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{Significance.} The published TCN generalizes across the dataset's payload, speed and altitude grid much
better than we anticipated in the proposal. A planner can trust it on unseen grid points, but not yet on genuinely
new conditions (route, wind). \textbf{Methodology matters.} Without the size-matched control and unseen-flight
scoring, a random-split model would be scored on flights it trained on. The resulting ``gap'' would mix a
condition shift, less training data and training error. \textbf{Challenges and limitations.} (i) Small $n$: some
comparisons rest on 8--21 flights, and the seed spread is as large as most gaps. (ii) The grid is coarse: three payloads
and five speeds, and a held-out level is still bracketed by measured airspeed. (iii) The wind estimate is
noisy: legs of the same flight disagree by $\approx$4.5\,m/s, so it separates windy from calm days rather than
ranking flights finely. (iv) Liu's constants were fit by the authors on all flights, which slightly favors Liu.
(v) \textbf{Retrained LSTMs collapse.} 16 of our 30 LSTM runs end up predicting a constant. All 10 ReLU units in the
output head are inactive on every input, so the output equals the last layer's bias and no gradient reaches the
LSTM again. The remaining runs keep only 1--4 active units, so their validation MAPE swings by 3--6 points between
epochs. The TCN head also loses 7--9 of its 10 units, but never the last one. We attribute the difference to the
optimizer: with batch size 1, RMSprop takes about 90k steps per epoch, each roughly the size of the learning rate
and driven by a single window, against 2.8k averaged Adam steps for the TCN. Our port matches the Keras defaults,
and the authors' script saves snapshots every 50 flights rather than a final model, so we cannot tell whether
their runs were affected. We therefore reproduce the LSTMs from the authors' weights but report no LSTM gaps.
\textbf{Risk going forward.} A physics + TCN hybrid may not beat a TCN that already generalizes. We have
therefore reframed it around data efficiency and new conditions (below).

\section{Next steps}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Physics + TCN hybrid} $\hat y_t = P_{\mathrm{Liu}}(x_t) + g_\theta(x_{t-19:t})$, with Liu's constants
refit on each split's training flights. We will evaluate it as a learning curve (5--52 training flights, several
random subsets per size) and on
\texttt{route}/\texttt{wind}, where the pure TCN degrades most.
\item \textbf{Uncertainty:} a Gaussian variance head (negative log-likelihood loss), a deep ensemble
(from the 3 seeds we already train) and MC dropout. Report 90\% interval coverage on the held-out splits, then feed
the predictive distribution into the authors' CVaR pipeline.
\item \textbf{LSTM training and completeness:} make LSTM training reliable (a leaky ReLU head, a lower learning
rate or Adam), then rerun both LSTMs on every split with 3 seeds; test whether the wind gap survives a
date-matched control.
\end{enumerate}
The small transformer from our proposal is deferred: the stress test shows the TCN is not limited by
architecture on this grid.
